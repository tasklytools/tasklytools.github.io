How to Price Freelance Projects Without Undercharging

Undercharging is rarely one bad decision. It usually comes from an unclear scope, an optimistic hour estimate, and a discount added at the last minute.

9 min read Updated November 5, 2026 TasklyTools guide

Start with your rate floor

A project price needs a floor. That floor comes from the income you need, your business costs, tax, buffer, and realistic billable hours. If you have not calculated it yet, start with the freelance hourly rate guide.

The floor is not the price of every project. It is the point below which the work stops making business sense unless something else changes, such as the scope, timeline, or strategic value.

Separate strategy from habit. A deliberate lower price for a test project or a long-term retainer is different from routinely guessing low because the client might say no.

Choose a pricing model

Common freelance pricing models
ModelBest forMain risk
HourlyOpen-ended support, advisory work, or unclear scope that is billed transparently.Penalises efficiency and can reward slow delivery if the client is not careful.
Fixed projectA defined deliverable with clear review rounds and dependencies.Underestimating effort or allowing scope to expand without a change request.
Value-basedWork with a clear commercial outcome and enough context to price it responsibly.Overclaiming value or pricing without understanding the client's economics.
RetainerOngoing capacity, maintenance, or support with a defined monthly commitment.Unlimited requests creeping into a fixed monthly fee.

You can combine models. For example, use fixed pricing for a defined build and an hourly or monthly rate for extra support after launch.

Estimate the real scope

List the work by activity, not by a single vague deliverable. Include the time that is easy to forget:

  • Discovery, research, and planning.
  • Production or delivery time.
  • Project management and client communication.
  • Included review rounds and revisions.
  • Testing, handover, and documentation.
  • Admin, invoicing, and closeout.
  • A risk allowance for uncertain dependencies or review cycles.

Use past projects to check the estimate. If a similar project took 42 hours but you remember it as "about 30", the timesheet is more useful than the memory.

A project pricing example

Illustrative fixed-price quote

Planning rate floor: $85 per hour
Estimated delivery: 30 hours
Base project price: $2,550
Explicit risk allowance: 10% = $255
Quoted price: $2,805

Included: two review rounds, one final handover session, and the agreed deliverable set.
Not included: additional pages, new integrations, or copywriting beyond the supplied content.

The 10% allowance is not a secret markup. It is a planned response to uncertainty. If the project has a stable brief and no external dependencies, the allowance might be lower. If approval involves several stakeholders, it might be higher.

Talk about price clearly

Present the price with the scope and the decision. Avoid apologising for the number or burying it after a long preamble.

  • Connect price to outcome: what the client gets, when, and with what level of support.
  • Offer a real choice: a smaller scope at a lower price or a larger scope at a higher price.
  • Name the trade-off: faster delivery, extra rounds, and added deliverables have a cost.
  • Do not negotiate against yourself: wait for a concrete objection before changing the price.

If the budget is genuinely below your floor, decline or reduce the scope. A project that cannot cover its delivery cost is not made safer by winning it.

Undercharging traps

  • Free unlimited discovery. Set a clear boundary for pre-project calls and scoping work.
  • "Quick" changes. Small requests add up. Use a change process once the agreed scope is exceeded.
  • No deposit. A deposit reduces risk and confirms commitment, where appropriate for the project.
  • Rounding down. A small discount can erase the buffer that protects you from delays.
  • Comparing to a salary. Employees and independent businesses carry different costs and risks.
  • Pricing from the client's budget alone. The budget is a constraint, not a complete pricing method.
  • Forgetting tax and payment fees. Account for the real amount you will receive.

Quote checklist

  • Rate floor is current and based on your real costs and billable hours.
  • Scope is listed by deliverable and activity.
  • Included review rounds, dependencies, and exclusions are written down.
  • Risk allowance is explicit and proportionate.
  • Price, payment schedule, and validity period are clear.
  • Any discount is matched by a scope or timeline trade-off.
  • The next step is one clear approval action.

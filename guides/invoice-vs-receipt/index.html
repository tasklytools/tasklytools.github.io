Invoice vs. Receipt: Differences and When to Use Each

An invoice asks for payment. A receipt confirms that payment happened. Using the right document at the right time keeps your records and client communication clear.

6 min read Updated November 5, 2026 TasklyTools guide

The short definitions

An invoice is a request for payment. It lists what was delivered, the amount due, the due date, and how to pay. It is created before payment is received.

A receipt is proof that payment was received. It usually shows the amount paid, the payment date, the method, and a reference to the invoice or transaction. It is created after payment is confirmed.

For a simple one-off sale, the two documents can feel similar. In a business-to-business workflow, they serve different purposes and may be handled by different people.

A single document can sometimes satisfy both roles in a small transaction, but do not call an unpaid document a receipt. The label should match what has actually happened.

A payment timeline

  1. Quote approved. The client accepts the scope and price.
  2. Work delivered. The agreed deliverable or milestone is complete.
  3. Invoice issued. You send invoice 1042 with a due date and payment instructions.
  4. Payment received. The bank or payment provider confirms the funds.
  5. Receipt issued. You confirm the amount paid and reference invoice 1042.
  6. Records updated. The invoice is marked paid and the payment is matched in your accounts.

Do not issue a receipt before cleared funds. A pending transfer, an authorised card payment, or a promised payment is not the same as money received. Confirm the payment according to your provider's actual status.

Keep the records connected

The invoice and receipt should reference each other. That link makes reconciliation easier when a client asks a question months later.

  • Keep the invoice number on the receipt.
  • Record the payment date, amount, method, and any transaction reference.
  • If the payment is partial, show the amount paid and the remaining balance clearly.
  • If a refund or credit note is issued, keep it connected to the original invoice and payment.
  • Store the documents in a consistent folder or accounting system with the project record.

Your exact recordkeeping requirements depend on your location, business structure, and tax rules. Check the requirements that apply to you and keep records for the required period.

Common mistakes

  • Calling an invoice a receipt. It creates a false impression that payment has been received.
  • Sending a receipt for a pending payment. Wait for confirmed funds or the correct provider status.
  • Reusing document numbers. Each invoice and receipt needs its own traceable reference.
  • Leaving out the payment method. A receipt should show how the payment was made.
  • Ignoring partial payments. Show the balance rather than marking the whole invoice paid.
  • Not matching the documents. A receipt with no invoice reference is harder to audit.

Document checklist

  • Invoice has a unique number, issue date, due date, and amount due.
  • Invoice line items match the agreed scope and price.
  • Payment was confirmed before a receipt was created.
  • Receipt shows the amount paid, date, method, and invoice reference.
  • Partial payment or remaining balance is stated where relevant.
  • Both documents are stored with the project and accounting records.

How to Calculate Profit for a Small Business

Profit is not the same as the money left in your bank account. This guide separates the numbers so you can see what the business actually earns.

9 min read Updated November 5, 2026 TasklyTools guide

Start with the income statement, not the bank balance

A bank balance shows timing. Profit shows whether the business earned more than it spent during a period. You need both, but they answer different questions. Start by separating the main layers:

  • Revenue: the value of sales or services delivered in the period, before expenses.
  • Cost of goods sold (COGS): the direct cost of delivering what you sold, such as materials, contractor labour tied to a project, or payment processing directly tied to sales.
  • Gross profit: revenue minus COGS.
  • Operating expenses: the ongoing cost of running the business, such as software, rent, insurance, and admin support.
  • Net profit before tax: revenue minus all business expenses.

The key discipline is consistency. Pick a period, use the same categories each time, and do not move an expense between categories just to make a number look better.

A worked example

Imagine a small service business reviewing one month. The figures below are illustrative, not benchmarks.

Monthly profit example
LineAmount
Revenue$48,000
Cost of goods sold$18,000
Gross profit$30,000
Operating expenses$12,000
Marketing expenses$4,000
Other expenses$2,000
Net profit before tax$12,000

The calculation is:

Gross profit
$48,000 - $18,000 = $30,000

Net profit before tax
$48,000 - ($18,000 + $12,000 + $4,000 + $2,000) = $12,000

If the example business sets aside an estimated 20% for tax on positive profit, the tax estimate is $2,400 and the after-tax profit is $9,600. That is an estimate for planning, not tax advice.

Calculate the margins

Absolute profit is useful, but margin makes it easier to compare months, products, or price changes.

Gross margin
Gross profit / revenue
$30,000 / $48,000 = 62.5%

Net margin before tax
Net profit before tax / revenue
$12,000 / $48,000 = 25%

After-tax margin
$9,600 / $48,000 = 20%

A higher margin is not automatically better. A business may deliberately accept a lower margin to win a strategic account. What matters is that the trade-off is visible and intentional.

Find the break-even point

Break-even revenue is the sales needed to cover fixed expenses after accounting for the gross margin. In this simplified model, fixed expenses are operating, marketing, and other expenses.

Break-even revenue
Fixed expenses / gross margin ratio
$18,000 / 0.625 = $28,800

In the example, revenue above $28,800 would begin to cover fixed costs under those assumptions. This is not a cash-flow forecast. It assumes the gross margin and expense mix stay reasonably stable.

When break-even is not available: if gross profit is zero or negative, selling more does not automatically cover fixed costs. Fix the gross margin or cost structure first.

Build a monthly review routine

  1. Close the month. Make sure sales, refunds, and expenses are recorded in the correct period.
  2. Reconcile the bank and payment accounts. Match transactions so the records reflect what actually happened.
  3. Separate COGS from operating costs. Consistent categories make the gross margin meaningful.
  4. Calculate gross profit, net profit, and both margins. Compare them with the previous month and the same month last year if available.
  5. Review the largest changes. A revenue increase with a falling margin deserves a closer look than a small rounding difference.
  6. Choose one action. Adjust a price, renegotiate a supplier cost, reduce a low-value expense, or follow up on receivables.

Keep the review short enough to repeat. A 30-minute monthly habit is more useful than an elaborate annual analysis that arrives too late to change a decision.

Common mistakes

  • Treating owner draws as a business expense. A draw is a distribution of profit, not an operating cost. Record it separately.
  • Forgetting tax reserves. Profit before tax is not the same as money available to spend.
  • Mixing personal and business spending. It makes both bookkeeping and decision-making harder.
  • Ignoring refunds and chargebacks. Revenue should reflect the period after those adjustments are known.
  • Calling every cost "COGS". That distorts gross margin and hides the real cost of delivery.
  • Comparing revenue only. A larger month can still be less profitable if delivery costs rise faster.

Quick review checklist

  • Revenue and refunds are recorded for the same period.
  • COGS includes only direct delivery costs.
  • Operating, marketing, and other expenses are categorised consistently.
  • Gross profit, net profit, and margins have been recalculated.
  • Tax reserve and owner draws are handled separately from expenses.
  • Break-even assumptions are written down and reviewed.
  • The review ends with one specific action.

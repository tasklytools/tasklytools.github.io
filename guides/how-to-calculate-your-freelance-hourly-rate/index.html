How to Calculate Your Freelance Hourly Rate

A freelance rate is not a salary divided by 2,080 hours. It has to cover tax, business costs, non-billable time, and the uneven reality of client work.

8 min read Updated November 5, 2026 TasklyTools guide

Why salary math falls short

Dividing a desired salary by 2,080 hours assumes that every working hour is billable and that someone else covers tax, software, equipment, insurance, sales time, and admin. Neither is true for most freelancers.

A sustainable rate therefore starts with the income you need to take home, then adds the costs of running the business and the time you cannot invoice. The result is a target revenue figure, not a wishful hourly number.

Use estimates, then update them. Your first rate is a hypothesis. Review it after a few months of real project data, especially the hours you actually spend on delivery and admin.

The rate formula

1. Pre-tax income need
Take-home goal / (1 - tax rate)

2. Base revenue
Pre-tax income need + annual business expenses

3. Target revenue
Base revenue + buffer

4. Billable hours
Working weeks x hours per week x billable utilization

5. Hourly rate
Target revenue / billable hours

The tax rate is an estimate for planning. It is not a substitute for advice from a qualified tax professional in your jurisdiction.

A worked example

Suppose you want to take home $60,000, estimate tax at 25%, expect $12,000 in annual business expenses, and want a 10% buffer. You plan to work 46 weeks per year, 40 hours per week, with 65% of those hours billable.

Rate calculation example
StepCalculationResult
Pre-tax income need$60,000 / (1 - 0.25)$80,000
Base revenue$80,000 + $12,000$92,000
Buffer10% of $92,000$9,200
Target revenue$92,000 + $9,200$101,200
Billable hours46 x 40 x 0.651,196 hours
Hourly rate$101,200 / 1,196$84.62

That does not mean every quote should be exactly $84.62 per hour. It gives you a floor for planning and a starting point for project pricing.

Choose realistic billable hours

Billable utilization is the share of working time you can invoice. Sales calls, proposals, admin, learning, invoicing, and internal meetings are usually not billable. A utilization figure near 100% is rarely realistic for a solo business.

  • Count actual working weeks. Subtract holidays, sick time, and planned breaks.
  • Count actual working hours. Do not assume every week is a full 40-hour client week.
  • Use a utilization figure you have observed. If you do not have data yet, start conservatively and revise it.
  • Test the result against your calendar. A rate that requires impossible billable hours is not a workable rate.

Turn the rate into a project price

For a fixed project, estimate the delivery hours and multiply by the hourly floor. Then account for risk and scope explicitly rather than hiding a random markup.

Example project quote

Estimated delivery: 30 hours
Hourly planning rate: $84.62
Base project price: $2,538.60
Explicit risk allowance for uncertain review cycles: 10% = $253.86
Quoted project price: $2,792.46

If the client asks for a lower price, reduce the scope rather than silently cutting your rate. Fewer deliverables, fewer review rounds, or a later start date are concrete trade-offs. For more on that conversation, read How to price freelance projects.

Common mistakes

  • Using last year's rate without reviewing costs. Software, tax, and living costs change.
  • Assuming full utilization. Non-billable work is part of the job.
  • Forgetting the buffer. Slow months and late payments are normal business risks.
  • Pricing a project from a guess. Estimate hours by task and compare with past projects.
  • Discounting without changing scope. That quietly lowers your effective rate.
  • Comparing your rate with an employee salary. The cost structures are different.

Rate review checklist

  • Take-home income goal is current.
  • Tax estimate is documented and reviewed with a qualified professional where needed.
  • Annual business expenses include software, equipment, insurance, and admin costs.
  • Buffer reflects slow periods and payment risk.
  • Working weeks, hours, and utilization are based on realistic data.
  • Project quotes use estimated hours plus an explicit risk allowance.
  • Discounts are matched with a scope change.
